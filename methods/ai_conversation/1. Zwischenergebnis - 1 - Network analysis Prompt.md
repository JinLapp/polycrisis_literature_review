# 1. Context / Introduction to the Topic

Contemporary societies are increasingly confronted with multiple global crises that interact across ecological, economic, technological, geopolitical, and social domains. Climate change, biodiversity loss, resource depletion, water scarcity, geopolitical instability, financial fragility, migration pressures, pandemics, energy transitions, and growing social inequalities are often treated as separate policy problems. However, a growing body of research argues that these crises are deeply interconnected and mutually reinforcing.

The term **polycrisis** has emerged as a broad umbrella concept describing situations in which multiple crises interact dynamically in ways that amplify systemic instability and make isolated solutions insufficient. In this research program, the term is used intentionally as a **generic descriptive concept** rather than as a fixed theoretical paradigm. It primarily refers to the existence of interconnected crises whose interactions generate emergent systemic effects across scales.

The increasing relevance of polycrisis research reflects broader developments in:

- systems thinking,
- Earth system science,
- sustainability science,
- resilience research,
- complexity theory,
- transition studies,
- governance research,
- and systemic risk analysis.

These fields increasingly recognize that global challenges cannot be adequately understood through isolated disciplinary perspectives alone. Ecological, technological, political, and socio-economic systems are deeply interdependent, nonlinear, and co-evolving. Interventions in one domain may unintentionally intensify problems in another domain.

For example, the large-scale electrification of transportation may contribute to climate mitigation while simultaneously increasing pressure on mineral extraction, geopolitical dependencies, ecological degradation, and global supply chain vulnerabilities. Similar cross-domain interactions can be observed in food systems, energy systems, digital infrastructures, migration dynamics, financial systems, and global governance structures.

The polycrisis perspective therefore emphasizes:

- systemic interdependencies,
- feedback loops,
- cascading effects,
- tipping points,
- multi-scale interactions,
- and long-term planetary dynamics.

At the same time, the field remains highly fragmented. Relevant research is distributed across numerous disciplines, methodologies, governance initiatives, modeling traditions, and conceptual frameworks. This research program aims to map, synthesize, and analyze these emerging approaches.

---

# 2. Core Idea of the Research Program

The core idea of this research program is to investigate how researchers, institutions, initiatives, and interdisciplinary fields conceptualize, study, model, and address interconnected global crises under conditions of systemic complexity.

The program is not primarily normative in the sense of prescribing what societies should do. Instead, it focuses on:

- how polycrisis phenomena are understood,
- how systemic interactions are conceptualized,
- which methodologies are used,
- which governance and transformation proposals are developed,
- and how different research traditions approach interconnected global risks and systemic transformations.

The program combines two complementary perspectives:

## A. Foundational / Analytical Perspective

This perspective focuses on:

- understanding systemic interactions,
- modeling interdependencies,
- analyzing cascading risks,
- studying feedback loops and tipping points,
- and conceptualizing planetary-scale complexity.

It examines how researchers describe and explain interconnected crises across domains and scales.

---

## B. Transformative / Solution-Oriented Perspective

This perspective focuses on:

- proposed governance approaches,
- institutional designs,
- sustainability transformation concepts,
- transition frameworks,
- resilience strategies,
- and systemic intervention models.

The focus is not on deciding politically “what should be done,” but on analyzing which forms of systemic solutions and transformation concepts are being proposed within existing research fields and initiatives.

# 5. Preliminary Overall Assessment

The emerging field of polycrisis research does not constitute a single unified discipline. Rather, it appears to be a highly interdisciplinary and evolving meta-field connecting:

- systems science,
- Earth system science,
- sustainability science,
- governance research,
- resilience studies,
- transition studies,
- complexity theory,
- political ecology,
- and systemic risk analysis.

The common denominator across these approaches is the recognition that global crises cannot be adequately understood or addressed in isolation because ecological, economic, technological, social, and political systems are deeply interconnected and dynamically co-evolving.

A major challenge for future research lies not only in identifying individual crises, but in developing conceptual, methodological, and governance approaches capable of understanding systemic interactions, long-term planetary dynamics, and the unintended consequences of interventions within complex global systems.









# Research Design: Network Analysis of Polycrisis Research


## Topic

**Polycrisis Research: Understanding Interconnected Global Crises Through Systems-Oriented and Interdisciplinary Perspectives**

## Purpose of This Document

This document translates the broad network-analysis framework into a more focused research design for the study of polycrisis-related research. It deliberately reduces precision in the node and edge taxonomy in order to preserve breadth and exploratory openness.

The design is intended for an early-stage field-mapping project. It should help identify the main actors, institutions, concepts, fields, key publications, methods, infrastructures, initiatives, and governance frameworks that structure the emerging research landscape around interconnected global crises.

---

# 1. Overall Research Aim

The goal is to map and analyze the emerging research landscape around **polycrisis and interconnected global crises**.

The study should not only ask:

> Who uses the term "polycrisis"?

It should ask more broadly:

> Which actors, institutions, research fields, concepts, and methodological traditions are shaping the study of interconnected global crises?

This distinction is important because many relevant communities may not use the word **polycrisis** directly. Researchers in resilience theory, Earth system science, systemic risk, sustainability transitions, complexity research, or cascading-risk analysis may study similar phenomena under different conceptual vocabularies.

The object of analysis is therefore not one fixed discipline. It is a **loose, interdisciplinary research constellation**.

---

# 2. Type of Study

The project can be defined as a:

> **Heterogeneous, exploratory, semi-systematic research-field mapping using network and graph analysis.**

This means:

- **Heterogeneous**: the graph contains different types of nodes, such as persons, institutions, concepts, research fields, key publications, methods, infrastructures, and initiatives.
- **Exploratory**: the field is emerging, fragmented, and not yet clearly institutionalized.
- **Semi-systematic**: the project uses structured search and coding, but allows snowballing and expert judgment.
- **Research-field mapping**: the aim is to understand the structure of a field, not to prove one narrow causal hypothesis.
- **Network and graph analysis**: the study focuses on relations between actors, ideas, institutions, outputs, and methods.

The design should prioritize **breadth first** and add precision later where important clusters become visible.

---

# 3. Main Research Questions

## 3.1 Central Research Question

> How is the emerging polycrisis-related research landscape structured across actors, institutions, research fields, concepts, and methodological traditions?

## 3.2 Subquestions

### Conceptual Structure

- Which core concepts organize the field?
- Which concepts connect otherwise separate research traditions?
- How are concepts such as systemic risk, resilience, cascading crises, tipping points, planetary boundaries, transformation, and governance connected?

### Field Structure

- Which research areas are most central to polycrisis-related thinking?
- Which traditions are close to each other, and which remain separate?
- Is there one emerging polycrisis field, or several loosely connected subfields?

### Actor and Institution Structure

- Which researchers, institutions, initiatives, and networks are central?
- Which institutions act as bridges between different traditions?
- Are there dominant hubs, such as research centers, international initiatives, think tanks, or global research networks?

### Methodological Structure

- Which broad methodological approaches are used?
- Are certain concepts strongly associated with certain methods?
- Are systems modeling, scenario analysis, network analysis, resilience assessment, and foresight connected or institutionally separated?

### Governance and Transformation Structure

- Which solution-oriented frameworks appear in the field?
- How are SDGs, Earth system governance, just transition, circular economy, doughnut economics, and planetary stewardship connected to polycrisis-related research?
- Are there coherent policy or transformation programs based on a holistic polycrisis view?

# 4. Scope and Exclusions

## 4.1 Core Scope

The core of the study should include:

1. **Human actors**
2. **Institutions and organizations**
3. **Concepts, ideas, and epistemic objects**
4. **Research areas and topic structures**

These are the main analytical layers.

## 4.2 Secondary Scope

The following categories should be included, but at lower resolution:

5. **Key publications**
6. **Methods and analytical approaches**
7. **Data, infrastructure, and technical resources**
8. **Research initiatives and funding programs**
9. **Policy and governance frameworks**

## 4.3 Excluded or Minimal Scope

For the first-stage graph, minimize or exclude:

- public discourse,
- mass media,
- scandals,
- public events,
- elections,
- social media dynamics,
- activist discourse,
- general public opinion.

These aspects may become relevant later, but they should not be central in the first-stage field map unless a specific public actor, public controversy, or media channel turns out to be structurally important.

# 17. Possible Hypotheses / Expectations

The study does not need strong causal hypotheses, but guiding expectations are useful.

## Hypothesis 1: The Field Is Fragmented

Polycrisis-related research likely does not form one unified field. It probably consists of several adjacent clusters.

## Hypothesis 2: "Polycrisis" Is Not the Central Concept Everywhere

The term may be visible in public-intellectual, policy, and synthetic writing, while adjacent research communities use concepts such as systemic risk, resilience, cascading failures, or planetary boundaries.

## Hypothesis 3: Institutions Matter More Than Journals

Because the field is interdisciplinary, research centers, initiatives, and networks may structure the field more strongly than disciplinary journals.

## Hypothesis 4: Bridge Concepts Are More Important Than Disciplinary Labels

Concepts like resilience, transformation, tipping points, systemic risk, and planetary boundaries may connect fields more effectively than formal disciplines.

## Hypothesis 5: Governance Is Present but Not Fully Integrated

There may be many governance frameworks, but few fully holistic policy programs that directly address polycrisis as interconnected crisis dynamics.

## Hypothesis 6: Methodological Fragmentation Is Likely

Modeling communities, governance researchers, critical theorists, and transition scholars may share concerns but use very different methods.

# 21. What to Avoid in the First Stage

Avoid the following at the beginning:

```text

social media analysis
detailed policy tracing
fine-grained method taxonomy

claims about hidden influence
over-interpretation of weak ties
```

Especially avoid graph conclusions such as:

```text

Institution X controls field Y
Funder Z determines concept Q
```

unless the evidence is strong.

Safer interpretations are:

```text
Actor A is structurally central.
Institution X connects several clusters.
Funder Z is visibly linked to several initiatives.
Concept Q appears across multiple fields.
```

# 23. Final Recommended Focus

For this topic, the study should primarily examine:

```text
the structure of an emerging interdisciplinary knowledge field
```

It should not primarily examine:

```text
public discourse
policy conflict
media attention
political polarization
```

The core analytical object should be the relationship between:

```text
actors
institutions
research fields
concepts
methods
key publications
initiatives
```

The central empirical question becomes:

> Is polycrisis research becoming a coherent field, or is it better understood as a loose intersection of several older research traditions?

The most important graph outcome would be a map showing whether the field is organized around:

```text
one emerging center,
several connected clusters,
or many weakly connected islands.
```

This gives both scientific and strategic insight: it shows where knowledge already converges, where it remains fragmented, and where future interdisciplinary work could have the greatest value.
